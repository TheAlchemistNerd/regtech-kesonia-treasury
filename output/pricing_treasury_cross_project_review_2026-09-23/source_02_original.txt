Funds Transfer Pricing (FTP): The Internal Capital Toll RoadFTP is an internal accounting framework where the treasury acts as a "central bank" inside the firm. It buys deposits from the retail branch and sells funding to the commercial loan department. The forward curve is essential for pricing complex, floating-rate, or multi-year structures. Locking the Cost of Future Funding: Imagine a commercial loan officer wants to approve a credit line that allows a corporate client to draw down Ksh 100M in exactly 1 year for a duration of 2 years. To charge the client correctly today, the treasury looks at the 1Y2Y forward rate (the 2-year rate starting 1 year from now). This forward rate becomes the baseline FTP funding charge to that loan department. Stripping Out Interest Rate Risk: If a branch originates a floating-rate loan that resets every year for 3 years, they are exposed to changing interest rates. The treasury uses the implied forward rates from the swap curve to calculate a single weighted FTP match-funded rate. This transfers the interest rate risk from the commercial loan officer straight to the treasury desk, leaving the loan team to focus purely on managing credit margins. 📈 2. Hedging Strategies: Neutralizing the Balance SheetOnce the treasury has absorbed all the interest rate risks from the bank's various branches via FTP, it uses the forward curve to construct market hedges using derivatives. Constructing Interest Rate Swaps (IRS): To hedge fixed-rate liabilities (like long-term certificates of deposit) against floating-rate assets, the treasury enters into interest rate swaps. The treasury uses the forward curve to calculate the expected future floating cash flows (e.g., 3-month SOFR or Euribor resets) to ensure that the net present value (NPV) of the swap is structured optimally. Executing Forward Rate Agreements (FRAs): If the forward curve indicates that short-term funding costs are going to spike significantly in Q3, the treasury will buy FRAs. This allows the bank to lock in a specific borrowing rate for a future window, protecting its net interest margin (NIM) from unexpected central bank hikes. 📊 Visualizing the Forward ShiftWhen the spot curve is upward-sloping, the forward curve sits above it. Hedging the $730M Gap with Interest Rate Swaps (IRS)In Scenario B, the bank has a major duration mismatch: long-duration fixed-rate assets are paired with short-duration liabilities. How the Derivative Neutralizes the Risk The Swap Position: The bank pays a fixed rate (e.g., 4.5% matching the duration of its loans) to a counterparty and receives a floating rate (e.g., 3-Month SOFR matching its flighty deposits). When Rates Rise: The value of the bank's fixed-rate loans drops. Dual Modeling: Deposits vs. Mortgage Prepayment RisksManaging a bank treasury means balancing a double-edged sword: you must model consumer behavior on both sides of the balance sheet simultaneously. Both deposits and mortgages contain embedded customer options, but they react in completely opposite directions to rate changes. A. Mortgage Prepayment Risk (The Asset Side) Homeowners have a free option to pay off their mortgages early (refinance) when market rates drop. Treasuries track this using the Constant Prepayment Rate (CPR): When Rates Fall: Homeowners rush to refinance. The bank's high-yielding fixed mortgages disappear early, forcing the treasury to reinvest that cash at lower, current market rates (Extension Risk in Reverse / Compression). When Rates Rise: Homeowners hold onto their low-interest mortgages forever. The bank's asset duration artificially stretches out from 4 years to 7 years (Extension Risk), exactly when the bank wants cash to invest at higher rates. B. The Asset-Liability Squeeze When interest rates shoot upward, a treasury gets hit from both sides at once due to consumer behavioral choices: Assets Extend: Mortgage prepayments dry up entirely. OAS is the definitive metric treasuries use to strip away the noise of that embedded option and find the true risk premium of a mortgage portfolio. 💡 1. The Core Concept: What is Option-Adjusted Spread?When a bank buys or originates a mortgage portfolio, the yield it receives isn't a clean indicator of value. If market interest rates drop, homeowners will prepay their loans to refinance at lower rates, truncating your high-yield cash flows. If you look at the raw Nominal Spread (Mortgage Yield minus Treasury Yield), you are ignoring this risk. OAS corrects this by calculating the constant spread added to the risk-free curve that equalizes the theoretical price of the mortgage with its actual market price, across hundreds of simulated interest rate paths. Nominal Spread = True Credit/Liquidity Premium (OAS) + Cost of the Homeowner's Option
Therefore, if you want to know the true return you are earning for taking on credit and liquidity risk, you must isolate the OAS: OAS = Nominal Spread - Option Cost
🧮 2. The Step-by-Step OAS Calculation ProcessTo calculate the OAS of a mortgage-backed security (MBS) or loan portfolio, the treasury's quantitative models run a four-step simulation: Step 1: Generate Interest Rate Paths Using an interest rate model (like the Black-Karasinski or Hull-White model), the software generates hundreds of distinct interest rate paths over a multi-year horizon. Some paths show rates spiking, some show them collapsing, and others show them twisting. Step 2: Overlay a Behavioral Prepayment Model For every single month on every single interest rate path, the model calculates an expected Constant Prepayment Rate (CPR). If a simulated path shows interest rates dropping 200 bps below the mortgage's coupon rate, the prepayment model projects a massive spike in CPR (e.g., jumping from 5% to 35% CPR) as homeowners refinance.This changes the expected cash flows (\[CF_{t}\]) for that specific path. Step 3: Discount the Path-Specific Cash Flows The model takes the cash flows from each path and discounts them back to the present day using the risk-free spot rate curve plus a trial spread (K).\[\text{Present\ Value\ (Path\ }i)=\sum \frac{CF_{t,i}}{(1+\text{Spot}_{t}+K)^{t}}\]Step 4: Iterative Solving for OAS The software averages the present values from all simulated paths. It then uses an iterative mathematical solver to adjust the spread (K) until the average simulated present value exactly equals the current Market Price of the mortgage portfolio. The value of K that solves this equation is your Option-Adjusted Spread (OAS). 📊 Visualizing the Value SplitWhen interest rates become highly volatile, the Option Cost balloons, which causes the true value (OAS) of a mortgage portfolio to shrink if the nominal spread remains stagnant. 🗒 3. It looks high-yielding on paper, but its homeowners are highly sensitive to refinancing, meaning the bank is taking on massive extension/prepayment risk for very little real compensation. Dynamic Hedging Adjustments: If the market volatility spikes and the calculated Option Cost expands, the treasury desk knows the portfolio's effective duration has changed. How LCR Restricts the 3-Month Deposit TrancheUnder Basel III, the Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) requires banks to hold an amount of High-Quality Liquid Assets (HQLA) that equals or exceeds their total projected net cash outflows over a 30-day severe stress scenario. \[\text{LCR}=\frac{\text{Stock\ of\ HQLA}}{\text{Total\ Net\ Cash\ Outflows\ over\ 30\ Days}}\ge 100\%\]When the treasury identifies that 20% ($200M) of its deposit base is in the volatile 3-month tranche, the LCR strictly dictates how that money must be invested: The Run-Off Penalty: For the LCR denominator, regulators apply strict "run-off factors." Retail demand deposits might see a 5% to 10% assumed outflow, but corporate or uninsured deposits can face a 25% to 100% run-off factor under stress. The Investment Restriction: If the treasury takes that volatile $200M and invests it in a corporate loan or a high-yielding mortgage, it generates 0% HQLA credit. The bank's LCR will plummet. The HQLA Mandate: To remain compliant, the treasury must reinvest this volatile short-term tranche into assets that qualify as HQLA:Level 1 HQLA (0% Haircut): Central bank reserves and sovereign bonds (e.g., US Treasuries or local government debt).Level 2A/2B HQLA (15% to 50% Haircut): Qualifying corporate bonds or high-quality residential mortgage-backed securities (RMBS). Therefore, the volatile 3-month tranche cannot chase yield; it is structurally locked into low-yielding sovereign or central bank assets to serve as an immediate liquidity buffer. 🧮 2. The Math Behind the Prepayment Model (PSA Standard)In mortgage modeling, the Public Securities Association (PSA) standard is the universal benchmark used to express prepayment speeds. Rather than assuming a constant prepayment rate from day one, the PSA model accounts for the fact that new homeowners rarely refinance or move immediately ("seasoning"). The PSA benchmark assumes that the prepayment rate starts very low and rises linearly over the first 30 months of a mortgage's life, after which it plateaus. The PSA Pricing Math The standard baseline is defined as 100% PSA (or 100 PSA): Month 1: \(\text{CPR} = 0.2\%\)Month 2: \(\text{CPR} = 0.4\%\)Month \[t\] (up to Month 30): \(\text{CPR} = 0.2\% \times t\)Months 30 to 360: \(\text{CPR} = 6.0\%\) permanently. If a treasury notes that a mortgage portfolio is prepaying faster than normal due to a sudden drop in market rates, they might model it at 200% PSA (which means you simply multiply the CPR by 2). \[\text{If\ }t\le 30:\text{CPR}=2\times (0.2\%\times t)\]\[\text{If\ }t>30:\text{CPR}=2\times 6.0\%=12.0\%\]Converting CPR to SMM for Monthly Cash Flows To calculate the actual cash flows for an OAS model, the treasury converts the annualized CPR into a Single Monthly Mortality (SMM) rate, representing the percentage of the remaining principal expected to prepay that month: \[\text{SMM}=1-(1-\text{CPR})^{1/12}\]You can simulate how different PSA speeds drastically alter the monthly SMM using the interactive visualizer below. AI-generated. It represents the maximum mark-to-market drop before collateral can be legally seized:\[\text{RC}=\max (\text{V}-\text{C};0)\](Where \[V\] is the market value of the swap and \[C\] is the collateral held). B. Potential Future Exposure (PFE) — The Volatility Add-on PFE calculates how much more valuable (and risky) the contract could become over its remaining life due to market volatility. SA-CCR calculates this using an Aggregate Add-on multiplied by a Multiplier that discounts the exposure if the bank holds excess collateral. Supervisory Delta: SA-CCR looks at whether a swap is long or short and applies a specific delta modifier based on whether it is an option, a linear swap, or a forward rate agreement.Maturity Factors: It mathematically scales down short-term trades while penalizing unmargined, long-term derivatives. The Treasury Impact: The Squeeze on Capital Because SA-CCR applies a strict 1.4x multiplier to the sum of RC and PFE, directional, unmargined hedges cause EAD to skyrocket. This translates directly into higher Risk-Weighted Assets (RWA), forcing the bank to hold more Tier 1 Equity Capital just to maintain its regulatory capital ratios. To mitigate this, treasury desks have been forced to migrate almost all macro-hedges to Central Clearing Counterparties (CCPs), where multilateral netting reduces the EAD significantly. 📋 2. To smooth out this P&L volatility, the treasury valuation desk sets up a dedicated CVA Hedging Desk. 💡 1. The Core Objective of CVA HedgingAs interest rates swing or a counterparty's creditworthiness degrades, the bank's CVA moves up and down constantly. If Counterparty Credit Risk Spikes ➔ CVA Loss Deepens ➔ Bank P&L Drops
If Bank Buys a CDS Contract        ➔ CDS Value Rises   ➔ Bank P&L Gains (Offset)
The CVA desk has a specific mandate: build a market position using derivatives that gains value at the exact same rate the CVA loses value. This requires managing two distinct risks: Credit Spread Risk (Mkt Risk): Covered by buying protection via Credit Default Swaps (CDS).Interest Rate / Market Risk: Covered by trading offsetting Interest Rate Swaps (IRS). 🛡 2. Constructing the CDS Hedge PortfolioA Credit Default Swap behaves like an insurance policy on a corporate counterparty. The bank pays a regular quarterly premium (spread) to a CDS seller. If the counterparty defaults, the seller pays the bank the face value of the underlying debt. To calculate how much CDS protection to buy, the treasury must determine the derivative portfolio's sensitivity to a 1 basis point shift in the counterparty’s credit spread—a metric known as Credit Delta (or CS01). Step-by-Step Construction Architecture: A. Determine Credit Sensitivity (CS01) The quant desk recalculates the lifetime CVA equation by bumping the counterparty's credit curve up by 1 basis point (+0.0001). For our $100M portfolio, let's assume the calculation yields:\[\text{CVA\ Credit\ Delta\ (CS01)}=-\$12,500\](This means for every single basis point the counterparty's credit spread widens, the bank suffers a $12,500 valuation loss). B. Size the CDS Protection Notional The desk must find a matching liquid CDS contract on that specific counterparty (or a highly correlated credit index like CDX/iTraxx if a single-name contract isn't available). They look at the CDS Contract Delta (CDS CS01), which measures how much the insurance contract gains when spreads widen by 1 bp.If a 5-Year CDS contract on that counterparty has a contract CS01 of $450 per $1 Million of Notional, the treasury calculates the required hedge size: \[\text{Required\ CDS\ Notional}=\frac{\text{Portfolio\ CS01}}{\text{CDS\ Contract\ CS01}}=\frac{\$12,500}{\$450}\approx \mathbf{\$27.78}\text{\ Million}\]C. Execute the Position The treasury buys $27.78M notional of protection. Now, if the counterparty's financial health deteriorates and their credit spread widens by 50 bps: The bank's underlying portfolio CVA drops by: \(-50 \times \$12,500 = \mathbf{-\$625,000}\)The bank's CDS hedge position gains: \(+50 \times \$450 \times 27.78 = \mathbf{+\$625,050}\)Net P&L Volatility: Virtually $0. The accounting risk is neutralized. 📊 Visualizing the Dynamic Rebalancing LoopCVA hedging is not a "set-and-forget" trade. Because the underlying expected exposure (EE) changes as market interest rates move and time passes, the portfolio's CS01 is constantly shifting. ⚠️ 3. The Catch: Cross-Gamma Risk & Regulatory Capital ReliefWhile dynamic hedging protects the income statement from massive shocks, it introduces structural operational challenges: Wrong-Way Risk (WWR): If the bank buys CDS protection from Counterparty B to hedge its exposure to Counterparty A, but Counterparty A and B are highly correlated (e.g., two large investment banks), the hedge might fail during a systematic financial crash. The SA-CVA Capital Reward: Under Basel rules, banks that actively hedge their CS01 are granted massive capital relief. How the SA-CVA Capital Charge Framework Calculates RWAUnder the post-crisis Basel frameworks (specifically the finalized Basel III frameworks), the Standardised Approach for Credit Valuation Adjustment (SA-CVA) treats CVA risk as a standalone market risk entity. For an unhedged portfolio, the capital charge reflects the pure, unmitigated sensitivity of your counterparty credit spreads. The framework maps out a prescriptive, step-by-step mathematical path to calculate Risk-Weighted Assets (RWA): [Netting Set Sensitivities (FRTB Style)] ➔ [Bucket-Level Aggregation] ➔ [Portfolio CVA Capital Charge (K)] ➔ [RWA = K × 12.5]
Step A: Calculate Regulatory Credit Spread Sensitivities (Delta & Vega) The treasury desk must compute the sensitivity of the portfolio's CVA to a 1 basis point shift in the credit spread (\[s_{i}\]) of each counterparty \[i\]. This is done for various maturity buckets (0.5Y, 1Y, 3Y, 5Y, 10Y):\[\text{Delta}_{i,k}=\frac{\partial \text{CVA}}{\partial s_{i,k}}\]Step B: Apply Regulatory Weights and Aggregate into Risk Buckets The Basel text assigns counterparties into 7 distinct sectoral buckets (e.g., Sovereigns, Financial Institutions, Corporates by industry) and attaches a fixed supervisory risk weight (\[RW_{k}\]) representing volatility. For example, a high-grade sovereign might have an \[RW_{k}\] of 0.5%, while a high-yield corporate has an \[RW_{k}\] of 5.0% or higher. The weighted sensitivities (\(WS_{i,k} = RW_k \times \text{Delta}_{i,k}\)) are aggregated within each bucket \[k\] using a regulatory correlation matrix (\[\rho _{ij}\]) to account for diversification among counterparties in the same sector:\[K_{k}=\sqrt{\sum _{i}WS_{i,k}^{2}+\sum _{i}\sum _{j\ne i}\rho _{ij}WS_{i,k}WS_{j,k}}\]Step C: Global Portfolio Aggregation to Find Capital Charge (\[K\]) Once the capital requirements for each sectoral bucket (\[K_{k}\]) are established, they are aggregated globally across all buckets using a supervisory cross-bucket correlation factor (\[\gamma _{kl}\], typically set around 0.50):\[K_{\text{all}}=\sqrt{\sum _{k}K_{k}^{2}+\sum _{k}\sum _{l\ne k}\gamma _{kl}K_{k}K_{l}}\]Step D: The Multiplier and Conversion to RWA For an unhedged portfolio, the final capital charge \(K = m \times K_{\text{all}}\), where \[m\] is a supervisory multiplier (typically set at 1.0, but regulators can scale it up if a bank's internal models fail backtesting). To convert this absolute dollar capital requirement into Risk-Weighted Assets (RWA), the treasury must multiply the capital charge by the reciprocal of the minimum capital ratio (1 / 0.08 = 12.5):\[\text{RWA}_{\text{CVA}}\mathbf{=K\times 12.5}\]The Penalty of Going Unhedged: Because an unhedged portfolio does not have offsetting negative \[WS\] entries from Credit Default Swaps (CDS) in the formula, the numbers inside the square root remain unmitigated. The resulting \[K\] is massive, tying up significant Tier 1 capital. 📋 2. Managing Accounting Mismatches: IFRS 9 & Hedge AccountingAn Accounting Mismatch occurs when a bank treasury executes a perfect economic hedge, but standard accounting rules create artificial profit-and-loss (P&L) volatility on the income statement. The Root Cause under IFRS 9 The Loan (Asset): Typically held at Amortized Cost. Its balance sheet value changes slowly over time as principal is repaid. Fluctuations in market interest rates do not change its carrying value on the balance sheet day-to-day. The Swap (Derivative Hedge): Contractually forced to be categorized as Fair Value through Profit or Loss (FVTPL). It must be marked-to-market daily. If interest rates drop, the economic value of the fixed-rate loan increases, which perfectly offsets the cash loss on the Pay-Fixed Interest Rate Swap. However, under standard accounting, the loan's gain is hidden (amortized cost), while the swap's loss is splashed immediately across the current quarter's income statement. The bank looks unprofitable on paper despite being perfectly hedged. Without Hedge Accounting:  [Loan Value Change (Hidden)] + [Swap Value Loss (Recognized)] ➔ High Artificial P&L Volatility
With Fair Value Hedge:     [Loan Risk Gain (Recognized)] + [Swap Value Loss (Recognized)] ➔ Net Zero P&L Impact
How Treasury Resolves This: The Hedge Accounting Playbook To stop this artificial volatility, treasuries deploy IFRS 9 Hedge Accounting, specifically utilizing a Fair Value Hedge (FVH) or a Cash Flow Hedge (CFH): Dynamic StrategyOperational MechanismAccounting TreatmentFair Value Hedge (FVH)Used when hedging a fixed-rate loan.The treasury flags a specific portion of the loan book. The change in the fair value of the loan attributable to interest rate risk is pulled out and recognized in the P&L, perfectly offsetting the swap's mark-to-market move.Cash Flow Hedge (CFH)Used when hedging a floating-rate loan or expected future rollover funding.The mark-to-market gains or losses of the swap are temporarily diverted away from the P&L and parked in Other Comprehensive Income (OCI) (equity). They are only released into the P&L when the floating cash flows actually hit the books.Operational Hurdles: The 3 Pillars of IFRS 9 Compliance A treasury cannot simply claim it is hedging; the ALM desk must continuously maintain a strict compliance trail: Formal Documentation: On day one, the bank must formally document the risk management objective, the exact hedging instrument (the swap), and the hedged item (the specific loans). The Economic Relationship Test: The bank must prove that the hedge is highly effective—meaning the derivative and the loan move in opposite directions in response to the same risk. Hedge Ineffectiveness Ingestion: If the hedge is slightly imperfect (e.g., the swap is for $100M but loans prepay via the PSA standard down to $95M), the $5M mismatch is labeled as "hedge ineffectiveness" and must be recognized directly in the P&L. If you want to dive deeper into exam-focused applications, tell me:Are you looking at this from an FRM Part II operational standpoint (e.g., calculating credit bucket correlations)?Or a CFA Level II/III portfolio management lens (e.g., how hedge ineffectiveness impacts reported earnings metrics)?AI responses may include mistakes.
